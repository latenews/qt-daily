## Text (NIV)

**22** While they were enjoying themselves, some of the wicked men of the city surrounded the house. Pounding on the door, they shouted to the old man who owned the house, "Bring out the man who came to your house so we can have sex with him."
**23** The owner of the house went outside and said to them, "No, my friends, don't be so vile. Since this man is my guest, don't do this disgraceful thing.
**24** Look, here is my virgin daughter, and his concubine. I will bring them out to you now, and you can use them and do to them whatever you wish. But to this man, don't do such a disgraceful thing."
**25** But the men would not listen to him. So the man took his concubine and sent her outside to them, and they raped her and abused her throughout the night, and at dawn they let her go.
**26** At daybreak the woman went back to the house where her master was staying, fell down at the door and lay there until daylight.
**27** When her master got up in the morning and opened the door of the house and stepped out to continue on his way, there lay his concubine, fallen in the doorway of the house, with her hands on the threshold.
**28** He said to her, "Get up; let's go." But there was no answer. Then the man put her on his donkey and set out for home.
**29** When he reached home, he took a knife and cut up his concubine, limb by limb, into twelve parts and sent them into all the areas of Israel.
**30** Everyone who saw it said, "Such a thing has never been seen or done, not since the day the Israelites came up out of Egypt. Think about it! Consider it! Tell us what to do!"

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 22-26: The mob's demand, the old man's horrifying offer of the women instead, and the concubine's brutalization through the night
- Verses 27-30: The Levite's cold discovery and response, and the shocked national reaction his gruesome message provokes

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "disgraceful thing" (v. 23, 24) — the old man names the moral horror of the mob's demand twice, yet his own proposed solution is itself deeply disgraceful, exposing how thoroughly moral categories have collapsed
- "do to them whatever you wish" (v. 24) — chillingly echoes the earlier offer in Genesis 19 involving Lot and the men of Sodom, a deliberate literary parallel signaling total moral collapse
- "think about it! Consider it! Tell us what to do!" (v. 30) — a threefold imperative addressed directly to the reader as much as to Israel, refusing to let the horror pass without demanding reflection and response

**Contrast structure**
- The old man's earlier extravagant hospitality ("let me supply whatever you need," v. 20) vs. his willingness to sacrifice two women's safety to protect his male guest — hospitality's obligations are honored toward one guest while completely betraying two others
- The Levite's brief, flat command, "get up; let's go" (v. 28), spoken to a woman who cannot answer because she is dead or dying, vs. the elaborate, horrifying degree of his later action (v. 29) — coldness toward her suffering is followed by a dramatic, almost theatrical public response
- The narrator's restraint in describing the assault itself ("they raped her and abused her throughout the night," v. 25) vs. the extended, graphic detail given to the Levite's dismemberment of her body (v. 29) — the text's focus is arguably less on sensationalizing her suffering than on exposing the horror of what Israelite society had become
- National shock and demand for reflection ("such a thing has never been seen," v. 30) vs. the fact that a very similar sin (attempted assault, an offered substitute) had occurred earlier in Genesis among Gentiles at Sodom — Israel has now replicated, within its own covenant community, the very horror once associated with pagan wickedness

**Notable expressions**
- v. 24 the old man's proposal to hand over his own daughter and the concubine — one of the most morally disturbing verses in the Old Testament, included by the narrator not as an endorsement but as unflinching testimony to how far covenant community values had eroded when there was "no king" and no shared moral authority
- v. 28 "there was no answer" — an unbearably restrained, devastating notice of the woman's death (or near-death), delivered without any expression of the Levite's grief, horror, or even basic tenderness
- v. 30 the direct address to "Israel" (and by extension, the reader) to "think about it... consider it... tell us what to do" — the narrator refuses to supply an easy moral resolution, instead demanding the audience wrestle with the implications themselves

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the narrator include such a disturbing, morally repugnant episode without offering direct editorial condemnation of every actor within the story?
   → The book of Judges consistently uses restrained, unembellished narration to let horrifying events speak for themselves, trusting the reader to recognize the moral collapse being depicted rather than needing to be told explicitly; the surrounding refrain ("Israel had no king; everyone did as he saw fit") functions as the implicit verdict on the entire episode.

2. What is the significance of this scene's clear echo of Genesis 19 (Lot offering his daughters to the men of Sodom), occurring here among Israelites rather than among the notoriously wicked Sodomites?
   → It delivers a devastating verdict on Israel's spiritual condition: the very kind of depravity once associated exclusively with pagan Sodom has now been fully replicated within the covenant community itself, showing that Israel's moral state had degenerated to the level of the nations it was meant to be distinct from.

3. Why does the Levite's response to finding his concubine (v. 28) appear so strikingly cold and detached, especially compared to the elaborate, dramatic act that follows (v. 29)?
   → His flat, seemingly indifferent response, followed by using her body instrumentally to provoke a national reaction, suggests that even the story's ostensible "victim's advocate" treats her primarily as property or a means to an end rather than as a person whose suffering deserves grief or lament in her own right — the narrative's restraint here may itself be a quiet indictment of him.

4. Why does the narrator conclude this horrifying episode with a direct call to the audience to "think about it... consider it... tell us what to do" (v. 30), rather than simply moving on to the next event?
   → It insists that this account cannot be read passively; the reader is drawn into the same moral crisis Israel faced, forced to sit with the horror and consider what kind of response — repentance, justice, restructuring of leadership — such a moral collapse actually requires.

5. What does this entire episode suggest about the ultimate consequences of a society where, as repeatedly stated, "everyone did as he saw fit"?
   → It shows, in its most extreme and horrifying form, exactly where moral relativism without any shared covenantal authority or accountability inevitably leads — not merely inconvenience or disorder, but the utter dehumanization and sacrifice of the most vulnerable members of the community.

## Application

- Recognize that a community or culture untethered from shared moral accountability under God can degenerate into treating even the most vulnerable people as expendable, and take seriously my own responsibility to protect rather than sacrifice others for personal safety or convenience.
- Refuse to look away from difficult, disturbing realities—whether in Scripture or in life—simply because they are uncomfortable; genuine reflection, as this passage demands, requires sitting with hard truths rather than moving past them too quickly.
- Examine whether I have ever treated another person's suffering primarily as useful for my own purposes (as the Levite used his concubine's body) rather than responding to them with genuine grief, dignity, and advocacy.
- Take seriously the passage's implicit warning about a society "with no king" — a community lacking shared, godly accountability — and consider what shared accountability structures are present or missing in my own life and community.
