## Text (NIV)

**22** The Israelites said to Gideon, "Rule over us-you, your son and your grandson-because you have saved us out of the hand of Midian."
**23** But Gideon told them, "I will not rule over you, nor will my son rule over you. The LORD will rule over you."
**24** And he said, "I do have one request, that each of you give me an earring from your share of the plunder." (It was the custom of the Ishmaelites to wear gold earrings.)
**25** They answered, "We'll be glad to give them." So they spread out a garment, and each man threw a ring from his plunder onto it.
**26** The weight of the gold rings he asked for came to seventeen hundred shekels, not counting the ornaments, the pendants and the purple garments worn by the kings of Midian or the chains that were on their camels' necks.
**27** Gideon made the gold into an ephod, which he placed in Ophrah, his town. All Israel prostituted themselves by worshiping it there, and it became a snare to Gideon and his family.
**28** Thus Midian was subdued before the Israelites and did not raise its head again. During Gideon's lifetime, the land enjoyed peace forty years.
**29** Jerub-Baal son of Joash went back home to live.
**30** He had seventy sons of his own, for he had many wives.
**31** His concubine, who lived in Shechem, also bore him a son, whom he named Abimelech.
**32** Gideon son of Joash died at a good old age and was buried in the tomb of his father Joash in Ophrah of the Abiezrites.
**33** No sooner had Gideon died than the Israelites again prostituted themselves to the Baals. They set up Baal-Berith as their god and
**34** did not remember the LORD their God, who had rescued them from the hands of all their enemies on every side.
**35** They also failed to show kindness to the family of Jerub-Baal (that is, Gideon) for all the good things he had done for them.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 22-28: Gideon refuses kingship in the LORD's name, yet his request for gold produces an ephod that becomes a snare for Israel
- Verses 29-35: A summary of Gideon's household and death, followed by Israel's swift return to idolatry and ingratitude

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "rule over" (v. 22, 23, twice each) — Israel's offer and Gideon's refusal both use the same phrase, sharply contrasting human kingship with the LORD's kingship
- "prostituted themselves" (v. 27, 33) — the same phrase used for Israel's worship of Gideon's ephod is repeated for their worship of Baal-Berith after his death, linking the two failures as the same underlying sin
- "did not remember" (v. 34) — a blunt verdict on Israel's spiritual amnesia, despite the LORD having "rescued them from the hands of all their enemies on every side"

**Contrast structure**
- Gideon's verbally sound theology ("The LORD will rule over you," v. 23) vs. his practically compromising action (making an ephod that becomes "a snare to Gideon and his family," v. 27) — right words do not guarantee right actions
- Forty years of peace during Gideon's lifetime (v. 28) vs. immediate relapse into idolatry "no sooner had Gideon died" (v. 33) — the nation's faithfulness proves entirely dependent on a human leader's presence rather than on genuine, internalized devotion to the LORD
- Gideon's kindness and deliverance ("all the good things he had done for them," v. 35) vs. Israel's failure to show kindness to his family afterward — ingratitude follows quickly on the heels of forgetfulness toward God

**Notable expressions**
- v. 23 "The LORD will rule over you" — a theologically correct and admirable refusal of monarchy, especially remarkable given how easily power could have been seized after such a decisive victory
- v. 27 "it became a snare to Gideon and his family" — the ephod, likely intended as a memorial or worship aid, becomes exactly the kind of idolatrous trap the LORD had commanded Gideon to tear down in his own father's household (6:25-27), showing a troubling repetition of the very sin Gideon once destroyed
- v. 30-31 "seventy sons... also a son... Abimelech" — Gideon's many wives and this particular son from a concubine plant the seeds for the violent chapter that follows (chapter 9), showing how present compromises shape future disaster

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does Gideon's correct refusal of kingship ("The LORD will rule over you," v. 23) sit right next to his request that produces an idolatrous ephod (v. 24-27)?
   → It shows the gap that can exist between sound theological conviction and consistent practice; Gideon rightly resists one form of self-exaltation (political rule) while unknowingly falling into another (a religious object that draws worship to himself and his hometown).

2. Why might Gideon have wanted to make an ephod at all, and what does its eventual role as "a snare" suggest about good intentions gone wrong?
   → It may have begun as a memorial of the LORD's victory, similar to earlier altars Gideon built (6:24), but its concentration of enormous wealth and its placement in Gideon's own town allowed it to become an object of worship in itself — good intentions do not prevent idolatry when they are not carefully guarded.

3. Why does Israel's faithfulness ("the land enjoyed peace," v. 28) hold only "during Gideon's lifetime," collapsing the moment he dies (v. 33)?
   → It reveals that Israel's obedience during this period was tied to the presence of a strong human leader rather than to any deep, internalized covenant loyalty to the LORD himself — a recurring and troubling pattern throughout Judges.

4. What is the significance of the text explicitly stating that Israel "did not remember the LORD" (v. 34) who had rescued them, rather than simply saying they sinned?
   → It frames the root of Israel's idolatry as forgetfulness rather than mere rebellion — a failure of ongoing gratitude and memory that allowed old patterns of unfaithfulness to resurface as soon as the immediate reminder (Gideon's leadership) was removed.

5. Why does the chapter conclude by noting Israel's ingratitude toward Gideon's family (v. 35), seemingly a minor detail compared to their idolatry?
   → It shows that forgetting God and failing in loyalty to those who served God's purposes on their behalf often go together; a community that forgets the Giver of deliverance also tends to forget and mistreat the human instruments of that deliverance.

6. What warning does Gideon's household — many wives, seventy sons, and Abimelech born of a concubine — set up for the reader heading into the next chapter?
   → It shows how personal compromises in one generation (polygamy, an ephod, unaddressed family dynamics) create the conditions for greater disaster in the next, foreshadowing the violent chaos Abimelech will bring in chapter 9.

## Application

- Examine whether there is a gap in my own life between theologically correct convictions I affirm and practices I have allowed to continue that quietly compete for the same devotion belonging to God alone.
- Consider whether my own faithfulness depends more on external structures, leaders, or circumstances than on a genuinely internalized, remembered relationship with God — ask what would happen to my devotion if a key support were removed.
- Practice deliberate remembrance of what God has done for me, since forgetting, more than open rebellion, often opens the door to drifting back into old patterns.
- Consider what "small" compromises in my own household or habits today might be quietly setting up larger problems for the future, as Gideon's ephod and family choices did.

## Prayer

Lord, examine my heart for any gap between the sound convictions I profess and the practices I have allowed to continue that compete with my devotion to you alone. Help my faithfulness to be rooted in genuine, internalized love for you rather than dependent on circumstances, leaders, or supports that could be removed. Guard me from the quiet forgetfulness that so easily leads back into old patterns, and give me a heart that actively remembers and gives thanks for what you have done. Show me any small compromise today that could grow into a larger snare for those who come after me. Amen.
