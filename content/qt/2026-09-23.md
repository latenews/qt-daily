## Text (NIV)

**1** After the time of Abimelech a man of Issachar, Tola son of Puah, the son of Dodo, rose to save Israel. He lived in Shamir, in the hill country of Ephraim.
**2** He led Israel twenty-three years; then he died, and was buried in Shamir.
**3** He was followed by Jair of Gilead, who led Israel twenty-two years.
**4** He had thirty sons, who rode thirty donkeys. They controlled thirty towns in Gilead, which to this day are called Havvoth Jair.
**5** When Jair died, he was buried in Kamon.
**6** Again the Israelites did evil in the eyes of the LORD. They served the Baals and the Ashtoreths, and the gods of Aram, the gods of Sidon, the gods of Moab, the gods of the Ammonites and the gods of the Philistines. And because the Israelites forsook the LORD and no longer served him,
**7** he became angry with them. He sold them into the hands of the Philistines and the Ammonites,
**8** who that year shattered and crushed them. For eighteen years they oppressed all the Israelites on the east side of the Jordan in Gilead, the land of the Amorites.
**9** The Ammonites also crossed the Jordan to fight against Judah, Benjamin and the house of Ephraim; and Israel was in great distress.
**10** Then the Israelites cried out to the LORD, "We have sinned against you, forsaking our God and serving the Baals."
**11** The LORD replied, "When the Egyptians, the Amorites, the Ammonites, the Philistines,
**12** the Sidonians, the Amalekites and the Maonites oppressed you and you cried to me for help, did I not save you from their hands?
**13** But you have forsaken me and served other gods, so I will no longer save you.
**14** Go and cry out to the gods you have chosen. Let them save you when you are in trouble!"
**15** But the Israelites said to the LORD, "We have sinned. Do with us whatever you think best, but please rescue us now."
**16** Then they got rid of the foreign gods among them and served the LORD. And he could bear Israel's misery no longer.
**17** When the Ammonites were called to arms and camped in Gilead, the Israelites assembled and camped at Mizpah.
**18** The leaders of the people of Gilead said to each other, "Whoever will launch the attack against the Ammonites will be the head of all those living in Gilead."

## Observation

**Structure (3 parts)**
- Verses 1-5: A brief, peaceful interlude under two minor judges, Tola and Jair, with no cycle of oppression mentioned
- Verses 6-9: A new and unusually severe cycle begins — Israel serves gods from seven different nations, prompting the LORD's anger and a harsh double oppression
- Verses 10-18: An extended, remarkably direct exchange between Israel's confession and the LORD's initial refusal, followed by genuine repentance

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "the gods of" (v. 6, listed six times) — the sheer repetition of nations and their gods emphasizes the scale of Israel's spiritual unfaithfulness, far broader than in earlier cycles
- "cried out" / "cried to me" (v. 10, 12, 14) — repeated across the LORD's speech and Israel's confession, forming the backbone of this unusually extended dialogue
- "we have sinned" (v. 10, 15) — Israel's confession is stated twice, the second time following the LORD's refusal, showing a deepening rather than a superficial repentance

**Contrast structure**
- The LORD's list of seven past deliverances ("did I not save you from their hands?" v. 11-12) vs. Israel's current unfaithfulness serving the gods of those same or similar nations — a direct, almost courtroom-style presentation of the evidence against Israel
- The LORD's stated refusal ("I will no longer save you," v. 13) vs. his eventual response ("he could bear Israel's misery no longer," v. 16) — a rare, striking picture of divine reluctance and compassion in tension with justice
- Israel's first confession, still followed by no recorded change in behavior (v. 10) vs. their second confession accompanied by concrete action — "they got rid of the foreign gods" (v. 16) — showing the difference between words alone and repentance evidenced by action

**Notable expressions**
- v. 14 "Let them save you when you are in trouble" — a biting, almost sarcastic divine response, exposing the folly of trusting in gods that have no power to help, by inviting Israel to test that trust
- v. 15 "do with us whatever you think best, but please rescue us now" — an unusually humble and unconditional posture of surrender, withholding no demand except the plea for rescue itself
- v. 16 "he could bear Israel's misery no longer" — an extraordinary, emotionally vivid description of God's own internal response, portraying his compassion as something that visibly moves him, not merely a policy of automatic forgiveness

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the LORD initially refuse to save Israel this time (v. 13), when in previous cycles he seems to respond to their cry without such explicit resistance?
   → This cycle marks an escalation in Israel's unfaithfulness (serving the gods of seven nations rather than one or two), and the LORD's refusal forces Israel to confront the real, logical consequence of trusting other gods — refusing to save immediately is itself a form of severe mercy meant to provoke genuine repentance rather than habitual, empty confession.

2. What is the significance of the LORD's biting response, "Let them save you when you are in trouble" (v. 14)?
   → It exposes, through irony, the emptiness of the false gods Israel has chosen; the LORD is not merely punishing but revealing the absurdity of divided loyalty, inviting Israel to see for themselves that their substitutes cannot deliver.

3. Why does Israel's second statement — "we have sinned... but please rescue us now" (v. 15) — differ meaningfully from their first confession in verse 10, given that both use similar words?
   → The second confession is immediately followed by concrete action: "they got rid of the foreign gods among them" (v. 16). This shows the difference between confession as mere words and confession that produces visible change — genuine repentance is validated by what follows it.

4. What does it mean that "he could bear Israel's misery no longer" (v. 16), and how does this shape our understanding of God's justice and mercy together?
   → It reveals that God's justice (refusing to rescue an unrepentant, idolatrous people) is not cold indifference; his own compassion is genuinely moved by his people's suffering once real repentance occurs, showing that his justice and mercy are not opposites but work together according to his character.

5. Why does the passage end with the leaders of Gilead needing to negotiate leadership ("whoever will launch the attack... will be the head," v. 18) rather than the LORD directly appointing a deliverer as before?
   → It sets up the human search for a leader (which becomes Jephthah's story) and shows a period in which Israel, even after repentance, must still take practical, faith-filled initiative rather than expecting deliverance to fall entirely without any human response or decision.

## Application

- Take seriously that half-hearted or habitual confession, without any corresponding change in what I actually trust or "serve," is not yet full repentance; ask God to show me whether my confession is producing visible change.
- Consider what things function as "other gods" in my life whose emptiness I need to honestly confront, rather than continuing to divide my trust between them and the LORD.
- Do not mistake any sense of God's discipline or apparent silence as the end of the story; his refusal to immediately fix things may be aimed at provoking real repentance rather than abandoning me.
- Trust that God's compassion is real and can be moved by genuine repentance, even after seasons of serious, repeated unfaithfulness.

## Prayer

Lord, examine whether my confession of sin is producing real, visible change, or whether I keep repeating the same words while still dividing my trust between you and other things. Show me honestly what "other gods" I have been quietly serving, and give me the courage to get rid of them rather than merely apologizing for them. Thank you that your justice and your compassion are not opposed to each other, and that you are moved by genuine repentance even after long seasons of unfaithfulness. Teach me to take faithful, practical initiative in obedience rather than waiting passively for you to act without any response from me. Amen.
