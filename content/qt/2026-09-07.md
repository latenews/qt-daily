## Text (NIV)

**12** Once again the Israelites did evil in the eyes of the LORD, and because they did this evil the LORD gave Eglon king of Moab power over Israel.
**13** Getting the Ammonites and Amalekites to join him, Eglon came and attacked Israel, and they took possession of the City of Palms.
**14** The Israelites were subject to Eglon king of Moab for eighteen years.
**15** Again the Israelites cried out to the LORD, and he gave them a deliverer-Ehud, a left-handed man, the son of Gera the Benjamite. The Israelites sent him with tribute to Eglon king of Moab.
**16** Now Ehud had made a double-edged sword about a foot and a half long, which he strapped to his right thigh under his clothing.
**17** He presented the tribute to Eglon king of Moab, who was a very fat man.
**18** After Ehud had presented the tribute, he sent on their way the men who had carried it.
**19** At the idols near Gilgal he himself turned back and said, "I have a secret message for you, O king." The king said, "Quiet!" And all his attendants left him.
**20** Ehud then approached him while he was sitting alone in the upper room of his summer palace and said, "I have a message from God for you." As the king rose from his seat,
**21** Ehud reached with his left hand, drew the sword from his right thigh and plunged it into the king's belly.
**22** Even the handle sank in after the blade, which came out his back. Ehud did not pull the sword out, and the fat closed in over it.
**23** Then Ehud went out to the porch; he shut the doors of the upper room behind him and locked them.
**24** After he had gone, the servants came and found the doors of the upper room locked. They said, "He must be relieving himself in the inner room of the house."
**25** They waited to the point of embarrassment, but when he did not open the doors of the room, they took a key and unlocked them. There they saw their Lord fallen to the floor, dead.
**26** While they waited, Ehud got away. He passed by the idols and escaped to Seirah.
**27** When he arrived there, he blew a trumpet in the hill country of Ephraim, and the Israelites went down with him from the hills, with him leading them.
**28** "Follow me," he ordered, "for the LORD has given Moab, your enemy, into your hands." So they followed him down and, taking possession of the fords of the Jordan that led to Moab, they allowed no one to cross over.
**29** At that time they struck down about ten thousand Moabites, all vigorous and strong; not a man escaped.
**30** That day Moab was made subject to Israel, and the land had peace for eighty years.
**31** After Ehud came Shamgar son of Anath, who struck down six hundred Philistines with an oxgoad. He too saved Israel.

## Observation

**Structure (3 parts)**
- Verses 12-15: The cycle repeats — sin, oppression by Eglon of Moab for eighteen years, crying out, Ehud raised up
- Verses 16-26: The detailed, almost cinematic account of Ehud's assassination of Eglon and his escape
- Verses 27-31: The resulting military victory, eighty years of peace, and a brief note on Shamgar

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "left-handed" (v. 15) — an unusual, specific detail that becomes the very key to the plan, since guards would expect a weapon on the left thigh, not the right
- "gave... into" (v. 12, 28) — again the language of "handing over" frames both Israel's oppression and its deliverance as under the LORD's sovereign hand
- "he too saved Israel" (v. 31) — the same verb used for Othniel and Ehud is applied to Shamgar, even though his story is compressed into a single verse

**Contrast structure**
- Eglon "a very fat man" (v. 17) enthroned in his "summer palace" vs. Ehud, an obscure left-handed Benjamite sent merely to carry tribute — worldly power and comfort are no match for the LORD's chosen, unlikely instrument
- The servants' polite assumption ("he must be relieving himself," v. 24) vs. the grim reality behind the locked doors — human assumptions based on appearances delay the discovery of what God has already done
- Eighteen years of oppression (v. 14) vs. eighty years of peace (v. 30) — the LORD's deliverance is disproportionately generous compared to the length of the suffering

**Notable expressions**
- v. 16 "strapped to his right thigh" — Ehud hides his sword exactly where a right-handed guard's search would not think to look, showing the LORD using an overlooked trait (left-handedness) rather than removing Ehud's limitation
- v. 20 "I have a message from God for you" — deeply ironic; Eglon expects prophetic word and instead receives fatal judgment, and in a sense he does receive "a message from God"
- v. 31 "an oxgoad" — Shamgar's deliverance comes through a farming tool, not a weapon, underscoring that the LORD needs no impressive arsenal to save

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the narrator spend so much narrative detail on the mechanics of Ehud's assassination of Eglon, down to the fat closing over the blade?
   → The vivid, almost grotesque detail underlines how thoroughly and decisively the LORD's judgment falls on the oppressor, and it shows the deliverer using ordinary human cunning and courage as the vehicle for God's purposes — this is not a magical rescue but a real, risky act of faith.

2. What is the significance of Ehud's left-handedness, and why would the text bother to mention it at all?
   → What might have seemed like a minor or even disadvantageous trait in a right-handed world becomes precisely the means of Israel's deliverance. It suggests the LORD works through the particular, sometimes overlooked, characteristics of the people he calls, not despite them.

3. Why do the servants delay so long before checking on the king (vv. 24-25), and what does that delay accomplish in the story?
   → Their polite, face-saving assumption buys Ehud the time he needs to escape and rally Israel's forces at the fords of the Jordan. It illustrates how human pride and decorum can blind people to urgent reality — and how God can use even that blindness for his purposes.

4. Why is Shamgar's whole story compressed into a single verse, with almost no narrative detail compared to Ehud's account?
   → It shows that the value of a deliverer in Judges is not measured by the length of his story or the sophistication of his methods, but by whether the LORD used him to save Israel. Even a farmer with a cattle prod is enough when the LORD is the one saving.

5. What does the contrast between eighteen years of oppression and eighty years of peace suggest about the character of the LORD's deliverance?
   → It reveals that God's rescue is never merely proportional or minimal; his grace toward a repentant, crying-out people is lavish and disproportionate to what they have earned, foreshadowing the far greater rescue accomplished in Christ.

## Application

- Consider what "unlikely" trait, background, or limitation in my own life God might actually want to use rather than fix or hide — my weakness may be exactly where his purpose fits.
- Resist the temptation to explain away uncomfortable or urgent realities with polite assumptions, the way Eglon's servants did; be willing to act on what is actually true rather than what is socially convenient.
- Remember that the size of the tool (an oxgoad, a left hand, a single verse of a life) does not limit what God can do with it when it is offered to him.
- When God delivers me from something, notice and give thanks for how disproportionately generous his rescue tends to be compared to the length or size of the trouble.

## Prayer

Lord, thank you that you are not limited by the tools, traits, or reputations the world considers impressive — you can use a left hand, an oxgoad, or a single overlooked person to accomplish your purposes. Forgive me for the times I explain away uncomfortable truths with polite assumptions instead of facing them honestly. Thank you for the disproportionate generosity of your deliverance, giving far more peace than the suffering I endured seemed to warrant. Use whatever is particular about me — even what feels like a limitation — for your purposes, and make me willing to act in courage when you call. Amen.
