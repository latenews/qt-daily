## Text (NIV)

**22** After Abimelech had governed Israel three years,
**23** God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the citizens of Shechem, who acted treacherously against Abimelech.
**24** God did this in order that the crime against Jerub-Baal's seventy sons, the shedding of their blood, might be avenged on their brother Abimelech and on the citizens of Shechem, who had helped him murder his brothers.
**25** In opposition to him these citizens of Shechem set men on the hilltops to ambush and rob everyone who passed by, and this was reported to Abimelech.
**26** Now Gaal son of Ebed moved with his brothers into Shechem, and its citizens put their confidence in him.
**27** After they had gone out into the fields and gathered the grapes and trodden them, they held a festival in the temple of their god. While they were eating and drinking, they cursed Abimelech.
**28** Then Gaal son of Ebed said, "Who is Abimelech, and who is Shechem, that we should be subject to him? Isn't he Jerub-Baal's son, and isn't Zebul his deputy? Serve the men of Hamor, Shechem's father! Why should we serve Abimelech?
**29** If only this people were under my command! Then I would get rid of him. I would say to Abimelech, 'Call out your whole army!'"
**30** When Zebul the governor of the city heard what Gaal son of Ebed said, he was very angry.
**31** Under cover he sent messengers to Abimelech, saying, "Gaal son of Ebed and his brothers have come to Shechem and are stirring up the city against you.
**32** Now then, during the night you and your men should come and lie in wait in the fields.
**33** In the morning at sunrise, advance against the city. When Gaal and his men come out against you, do whatever your hand finds to do."
**34** So Abimelech and all his troops set out by night and took up concealed positions near Shechem in four companies.
**35** Now Gaal son of Ebed had gone out and was standing at the entrance to the city gate just as Abimelech and his soldiers came out from their hiding place.
**36** When Gaal saw them, he said to Zebul, "Look, people are coming down from the tops of the mountains!" Zebul replied, "You mistake the shadows of the mountains for men."
**37** But Gaal spoke up again: "Look, people are coming down from the center of the land, and a company is coming from the direction of the soothsayers' tree."
**38** Then Zebul said to him, "Where is your big talk now, you who said, 'Who is Abimelech that we should be subject to him?' Aren't these the men you ridiculed? Go out and fight them!"
**39** So Gaal led out the citizens of Shechem and fought Abimelech.
**40** Abimelech chased him, and many fell wounded in the flight-all the way to the entrance to the gate.
**41** Abimelech stayed in Arumah, and Zebul drove Gaal and his brothers out of Shechem.
**42** The next day the people of Shechem went out to the fields, and this was reported to Abimelech.
**43** So he took his men, divided them into three companies and set an ambush in the fields. When he saw the people coming out of the city, he rose to attack them.
**44** Abimelech and the companies with him rushed forward to a position at the entrance to the city gate. Then two companies rushed upon those in the fields and struck them down.
**45** All that day Abimelech pressed his attack against the city until he had captured it and killed its people. Then he destroyed the city and scattered salt over it.

## Observation

**Structure (3 parts)**
- Verses 22-25: God sends an evil spirit to unravel the corrupt alliance, and Shechem turns to open banditry and hostility against Abimelech
- Verses 26-41: Gaal's rebellion against Abimelech, Zebul's betrayal of Gaal, and the first defeat of Gaal's uprising
- Verses 42-45: Abimelech's brutal, total destruction of Shechem itself

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "treacherously" (v. 23) — named explicitly as the theme of what follows: betrayal answering betrayal, since Shechem had helped Abimelech betray and murder his own brothers
- "Who is Abimelech" (v. 28, echoed in v. 38) — Gaal's mocking rhetorical question is thrown back at him by Zebul once his bluff is called, in a pattern reminiscent of Succoth's earlier undoing (8:6, 15)
- "sent" (v. 23, of the evil spirit; v. 31, of Zebul's messengers) — human political scheming (Zebul's secret messengers) unfolds within a larger divine act (the evil spirit God sent), showing both operating simultaneously

**Contrast structure**
- Verse 24's stated purpose ("that the crime... might be avenged") vs. the seemingly ordinary political intrigue of vv. 26-41 — the narrator insists that behind Gaal's ambition, Zebul's anger, and Abimelech's fury lies God's own justice quietly working itself out through human treachery
- Gaal's bold public boasting ("who is Abimelech... I would get rid of him," vv. 28-29) vs. his humiliating defeat and expulsion (vv. 40-41) — big talk collapses quickly when actually tested
- Shechem's citizens who once "gathered beside the great tree... to crown Abimelech king" (9:6) vs. their eventual total destruction, "scattered salt over it" (v. 45) — the very city that made Abimelech king becomes the city he annihilates

**Notable expressions**
- v. 23 "God sent an evil spirit" — a striking and theologically difficult statement showing that God can use even corrupted, evil dynamics between wicked parties to accomplish his own purposes of justice, without becoming the author of their sin
- v. 36-37 Zebul's mocking dismissal of Gaal's accurate observations ("you mistake the shadows... for men") — a chilling example of manipulation, since Zebul already knows Abimelech is coming and deliberately delays Gaal's response until it is too late
- v. 45 "scattered salt over it" — a symbolic act signifying the city's total, deliberate desolation, intended to render the ground unfit for future habitation or growth

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. What does it mean that "God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the citizens of Shechem" (v. 23), and how should this be understood theologically?
   → It reflects the Old Testament's way of affirming God's ultimate sovereignty even over the outworking of human evil and treachery — God is not credited with creating evil intent, but he actively directs and permits the consequences of already-corrupted human relationships to accomplish his own just purposes.

2. Why does the narrator explicitly state the purpose behind this unraveling (v. 24) — vengeance for the seventy murdered brothers — before narrating the seemingly unrelated political conflict that follows?
   → It ensures the reader interprets everything that follows, however ordinary or politically motivated it appears on the surface, as part of God's moral accounting for the earlier massacre; nothing in the ensuing chaos is outside his just governance.

3. Why does Zebul deliberately delay confirming Gaal's accurate warnings about Abimelech's approaching army (vv. 36-37) until it is too late for effective response?
   → It shows calculated manipulation designed to humiliate and destroy Gaal specifically, using Gaal's own earlier boastful words against him at the worst possible moment, illustrating how political treachery breeds further treachery.

4. What does the totality of Abimelech's destruction of Shechem (v. 45), down to scattering salt over the ruins, reveal about the consequences of the violence set in motion since chapter 9 began?
   → It shows how the cycle of betrayal and violence, once begun with the murder of Gideon's seventy sons, does not simply resolve but escalates until it consumes even those who first enabled it — the city that made Abimelech king suffers total destruction at his hands.

5. What overall warning does this extended account of political treachery and violence offer about seeking or securing power through corrupt means?
   → It shows that alliances and kingships built on murder, manipulation, and self-interest are inherently unstable and tend to devour even their own architects and supporters — sin's cycle of violence rarely stops with its original victims.

## Application

- Trust that God remains sovereign and just even in situations of complicated human treachery, political conflict, or corruption that seem far removed from any obvious moral order.
- Be wary of alliances, ambitions, or shortcuts to influence and power built on manipulation or injustice; consider how such foundations tend to prove unstable over time.
- Examine whether I have ever used someone else's earlier words or mistakes against them for personal advantage, the way Zebul did with Gaal, and consider what true accountability without manipulation looks like.
- Take seriously that sin's consequences often extend beyond the original wrongdoers to those who enabled or benefited from it, and let that shape how seriously I take my own participation in group wrongdoing.
