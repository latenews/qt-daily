## Text (NIV)

**1** The angel of the LORD went up from Gilgal to Bokim and said, "I brought you up out of Egypt and led you into the land that I swore to give to your forefathers. I said, 'I will never break my covenant with you,
**2** and you shall not make a covenant with the people of this land, but you shall break down their altars.' Yet you have disobeyed me. Why have you done this?
**3** Now therefore I tell you that I will not drive them out before you; they will be thorns in your sides and their gods will be a snare to you."
**4** When the angel of the LORD had spoken these things to all the Israelites, the people wept aloud,
**5** and they called that place Bokim. There they offered sacrifices to the LORD.
**6** After Joshua had dismissed the Israelites, they went to take possession of the land, each to his own inheritance.
**7** The people served the LORD throughout the lifetime of Joshua and of the elders who outlived him and who had seen all the great things the LORD had done for Israel.
**8** Joshua son of Nun, the servant of the LORD, died at the age of a hundred and ten.
**9** And they buried him in the land of his inheritance, at Timnath Heres in the hill country of Ephraim, north of Mount Gaash.
**10** After that whole generation had been gathered to their fathers, another generation grew up, who knew neither the LORD nor what he had done for Israel.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 1-5: The angel of the LORD confronts Israel directly with their disobedience, resulting in weeping and sacrifice at a place they name Bokim
- Verses 6-10: A summary retrospective on Joshua's generation, his death, and the rise of a new generation with no direct knowledge of the LORD or his works

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "I brought you up... I said... you have disobeyed" (v. 1-2) — the angel's speech follows a clear pattern of recounting God's faithfulness before naming Israel's specific failure, a rhetorical structure repeated throughout the book (as in 6:8-10)
- "drive them out" (v. 3, echoing chapter 1's repeated refrain) — directly ties this confrontation to the specific list of failures just catalogued in chapter 1
- "knew neither the LORD nor what he had done" (v. 10) — a stark description of spiritual amnesia that sets up the entire cyclical pattern the rest of Judges will document

**Contrast structure**
- Israel's tearful, sacrificial response at Bokim (vv. 4-5) vs. the complete absence of any described change in behavior afterward — emotional response and true repentance are shown to be two different things, since the very next verses (chapter 2 continuing) show the cycle beginning anyway
- The generation that "served the LORD" because they had personally "seen all the great things the LORD had done" (v. 7) vs. the next generation who "knew neither the LORD nor what he had done" (v. 10) — direct experiential knowledge of God's works, once absent, is shown to be devastatingly difficult to transmit
- God's declared consequence ("I will not drive them out... they will be thorns," v. 3) vs. Israel's earlier explanations in chapter 1 (Canaanite determination, iron chariots) — this verse reframes what looked like tactical or circumstantial failure as, in truth, a specific divine judgment for disobedience

**Notable expressions**
- v. 1 "the angel of the LORD went up from Gilgal to Bokim" — Gilgal was the site of Israel's first camp and covenant renewal in the promised land (Joshua 4-5), making this journey to a place of weeping a symbolic reversal of that earlier hopeful beginning
- v. 3 "thorns in your sides... a snare to you" — vivid, physical imagery describing ongoing pain and entrapment rather than a single event, indicating this consequence would be a persistent condition, not a momentary setback
- v. 10 "knew neither the LORD nor what he had done" — pairs relational knowledge ("knew... the LORD") with historical knowledge ("what he had done"), suggesting both dimensions of faith were lost together in a single generational transition

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the angel of the LORD reframe Israel's incomplete conquest (already described in chapter 1 in terms of Canaanite resolve and iron chariots) explicitly as the direct consequence of Israel's own disobedience (vv. 2-3)?
   → It corrects any impression that the failures of chapter 1 were merely circumstantial or tactical; the deeper truth is that Israel's own covenant unfaithfulness — making agreements with the very peoples they were told to remove — is the real cause behind their ongoing struggle.

2. Why does Israel's tearful, sacrificial response at Bokim (vv. 4-5) not appear to produce any actual change in the nation's behavior in the chapters that follow?
   → It illustrates the difference between emotional remorse and genuine repentance that leads to changed action; grief over consequences is not the same as a transformed will to obey, a distinction the rest of Judges will illustrate repeatedly.

3. What does it mean that the generation which "served the LORD" did so specifically because they had "seen all the great things the LORD had done" (v. 7), and why is this significant for the generation that follows?
   → It highlights that firsthand experience of God's power and faithfulness was, for that generation, a primary anchor for their obedience; the loss of this direct experience in the next generation (v. 10) suggests how vulnerable faith can be when it depends solely on personal memory rather than being actively taught and transmitted.

4. Why is it so devastating that the next generation "knew neither the LORD nor what he had done for Israel" (v. 10), especially given how vividly and recently these events had occurred?
   → It shows how quickly foundational knowledge of God and his works can be lost within a single generational transition if it is not deliberately taught and passed on, regardless of how dramatic or recent the events themselves were.

5. How does this passage function as a hinge point explaining the entire cyclical pattern that will structure the rest of the book of Judges?
   → It identifies the root cause (a generation without direct knowledge of the LORD) that will produce the repeated cycle of sin, oppression, crying out, and deliverance seen throughout the book — the fundamental problem is not merely bad choices but a failure of spiritual transmission between generations.

## Application

- Examine whether my own grief or emotional response to conviction about sin (like Israel's weeping at Bokim) is producing genuine, lasting change in behavior, or whether it risks remaining only a passing emotional experience.
- Take seriously the responsibility to actively teach and transmit knowledge of what God has done — not simply assuming that faith or awareness of his works will automatically pass to the next generation without deliberate effort.
- Reflect on what specific things I have personally seen God do, and consider how I can put those experiences into words for others rather than keeping them as private memory alone.
- Recognize that circumstances I might attribute to external difficulty may, on deeper reflection, actually trace back to my own unaddressed disobedience, as Israel's incomplete conquest did.

## Prayer

And where I have blamed circumstances for struggles that may actually trace back to my own disobedience, give me honest eyes to see the deeper cause. Amen.
