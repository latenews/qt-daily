## Text (NIV)

**13** Now listen, you who say, "Today or tomorrow we will go to this or that city, spend a year there, carry on business and make money."
**14** Why, you do not even know what will happen tomorrow. What is your life? You are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes.
**15** Instead, you ought to say, "If it is the Lord's will, we will live and do this or that."
**16** As it is, you boast and brag. All such boasting is evil.
**17** Anyone, then, who knows the good he ought to do and doesn't do it, sins.

**1** Now listen, you rich people, weep and wail because of the misery that is coming upon you.
**2** Your wealth has rotted, and moths have eaten your clothes.
**3** Your gold and silver are corroded. Their corrosion will testify against you and eat your flesh like fire. You have hoarded wealth in the last days.
**4** Look! The wages you failed to pay the workmen who mowed your fields are crying out against you. The cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord Almighty.
**5** You have lived on earth in luxury and self-indulgence. You have fattened yourselves in the day of slaughter.
**6** You have condemned and murdered innocent men, who were not opposing you.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- 4:13-17: A warning against presumptuous planning that ignores both life's brevity and dependence on God's will
- 5:1-6: A severe pronouncement of judgment against the rich for hoarding wealth and exploiting workers

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "listen" (4:13, 5:1) — both sections open with the same direct, attention-demanding address, marking two related but distinct warnings
- "wealth... gold and silver... hoarded" (5:2-3) — repeated across multiple forms of accumulated riches, emphasizing the totality of what has been stored up unjustly
- "crying out" (5:4) — the unpaid workers' wages are personified as having a voice that reaches God directly, echoing the language of injustice crying out to God throughout the Old Testament (as with Abel's blood in Genesis 4)

**Contrast structure**
- Confident human planning, "we will go... spend a year... make money" (4:13) vs. the reality that "you do not even know what will happen tomorrow" (4:14) — presumptuous certainty exposed against genuine human limitation and ignorance of the future
- Life as "a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes" (4:14) vs. the rich who have "hoarded wealth in the last days" (5:3) as though it would last indefinitely — two different responses to life's brevity, one appropriately humble, one dangerously presumptuous
- The wages "you failed to pay" crying out (5:4) vs. those who "lived on earth in luxury and self-indulgence" (5:5) on the withheld wages of others — direct injustice toward workers funds indulgent excess for their employers
- "Fattened yourselves in the day of slaughter" (5:5) — the imagery of an animal unknowingly being fattened for its own destruction rather than for lasting benefit, capturing the ultimate futility of self-indulgent accumulation

**Notable expressions**
- 4:15 "if it is the Lord's will, we will live and do this or that" — a simple, humble alternative to presumptuous planning, acknowledging both the uncertainty of the future and genuine dependence on God's sovereignty
- 4:17 "anyone, then, who knows the good he ought to do and doesn't do it, sins" — broadens the definition of sin to include not only wrong actions but also known good left undone, a significant and often overlooked category
- 5:3 "their corrosion will testify against you and eat your flesh like fire" — vivid, almost apocalyptic imagery portraying hoarded, unused wealth as itself becoming evidence and instrument of judgment rather than remaining a source of security

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does James specifically target confident, detailed business planning ("today or tomorrow we will go... make money," 4:13) rather than planning in general?
   → It is not planning itself that is condemned, but planning that presumes total control over an uncertain future without any acknowledgment of dependence on God; the issue is the posture of self-sufficient confidence, not the act of thinking ahead.

2. What does it mean that "anyone who knows the good he ought to do and doesn't do it, sins" (4:17), and how does this relate to the surrounding discussion of presumptuous planning?
   → It suggests that recognizing life's brevity and dependence on God's will (4:14-15) is itself a "good" that, once known, obligates a response; failing to act on that knowledge — continuing in presumptuous self-reliance despite knowing better — constitutes a real, specific sin of omission.

3. Why does James address the rich with such severe, almost prophetic language ("weep and wail because of the misery that is coming," 5:1), reminiscent of Old Testament prophetic oracles of judgment?
   → It signals that this is not merely practical advice but a serious pronouncement of coming divine judgment, placing James's warning about wealth within the same prophetic tradition that consistently condemned the exploitation of the poor and vulnerable by the wealthy and powerful.

4. What is the significance of describing withheld wages as themselves "crying out" and reaching "the ears of the Lord Almighty" (5:4)?
   → It personifies economic injustice as something God directly hears and responds to, showing that mistreatment of workers is not merely a private business matter but a serious moral offense that God takes personally and will address.

5. What does the image of being "fattened... for the day of slaughter" (5:5) suggest about the ultimate outcome of a life devoted to self-indulgent accumulation?
   → It reveals the tragic irony of such a life — what feels like security and abundance in the present is actually preparation for judgment, since wealth accumulated through injustice and devoted only to self-indulgence provides no genuine lasting protection or benefit.

## Application

- Examine my own planning and ambitions for signs of presumptuous self-sufficiency, and practice genuinely acknowledging dependence on God's will rather than assuming control over an uncertain future.
- Reflect on any "good" I currently know I ought to do but have been neglecting, and take James's warning about sins of omission seriously rather than measuring my faithfulness only by wrongs avoided.
- If I have any authority over others' wages, compensation, or fair treatment, examine whether I am treating them justly and promptly, remembering that mistreatment of workers is something God hears and takes seriously.
- Consider whether any accumulation or indulgence in my own life reflects genuine stewardship or a dangerous, false sense of security that ignores life's brevity and coming accountability.

## Prayer

Lord, examine my planning and ambitions for presumptuous self-sufficiency, and teach me to genuinely depend on your will rather than assuming control over an uncertain future. Show me any good I already know to do but have been neglecting, and give me the will to act on it. If I have any authority over others' fair treatment or compensation, keep me from injustice, remembering that you hear the cries of those who are wronged. And examine my own relationship with wealth and accumulation, that it might reflect genuine stewardship rather than false security. Amen.
