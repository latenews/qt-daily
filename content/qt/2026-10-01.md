## Text (NIV)

**1** Later on, at the time of wheat harvest, Samson took a young goat and went to visit his wife. He said, "I'm going to my wife's room." But her father would not let him go in.
**2** "I was so sure you thoroughly hated her," he said, "that I gave her to your friend. Isn't her younger sister more attractive? Take her instead."
**3** Samson said to them, "This time I have a right to get even with the Philistines; I will really harm them."
**4** So he went out and caught three hundred foxes and tied them tail to tail in pairs. He then fastened a torch to every pair of tails,
**5** lit the torches and let the foxes loose in the standing grain of the Philistines. He burned up the shocks and standing grain, together with the vineyards and olive groves.
**6** When the Philistines asked, "Who did this?" they were told, "Samson, the Timnite's son-in-law, because his wife was given to his friend." So the Philistines went up and burned her and her father to death.
**7** Samson said to them, "Since you've acted like this, I won't stop until I get my revenge on you."
**8** He attacked them viciously and slaughtered many of them. Then he went down and stayed in a cave in the rock of Etam.
**9** The Philistines went up and camped in Judah, spreading out near Lehi.
**10** The men of Judah asked, "Why have you come to fight us?" "We have come to take Samson prisoner," they answered, "to do to him as he did to us."
**11** Then three thousand men from Judah went down to the cave in the rock of Etam and said to Samson, "Don't you realize that the Philistines are rulers over us? What have you done to us?" He answered, "I merely did to them what they did to me."
**12** They said to him, "We've come to tie you up and hand you over to the Philistines." Samson said, "Swear to me that you won't kill me yourselves."
**13** "Agreed," they answered. "We will only tie you up and hand you over to them. We will not kill you." So they bound him with two new ropes and led him up from the rock.
**14** As he approached Lehi, the Philistines came toward him shouting. The Spirit of the LORD came upon him in power. The ropes on his arms became like charred flax, and the bindings dropped from his hands.
**15** Finding a fresh jawbone of a donkey, he grabbed it and struck down a thousand men.
**16** Then Samson said, "With a donkey's jawbone I have made donkeys of them. With a donkey's jawbone I have killed a thousand men."
**17** When he finished speaking, he threw away the jawbone; and the place was called Ramath Lehi.
**18** Because he was very thirsty, he cried out to the LORD, "You have given your servant this great victory. Must I now die of thirst and fall into the hands of the uncircumcised?"
**19** Then God opened up the hollow place in Lehi, and water came out of it. When Samson drank, his strength returned and he revived. So the spring was called En Hakkore, and it is still there in Lehi.
**20** Samson led Israel for twenty years in the days of the Philistines.

## Observation

**Structure (3 parts)**
- Verses 1-8: A cycle of escalating personal revenge between Samson and the Philistines, beginning with a marital slight
- Verses 9-17: Judah's fearful compliance with the Philistines, Samson's Spirit-empowered breakout, and his defeat of a thousand men
- Verses 18-20: Samson's cry for water in his exhaustion and the LORD's provision, closing with a summary of his twenty-year leadership

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "get even" / "revenge" (v. 3, 7) — Samson's stated motive is explicitly personal retaliation, repeated and escalating with each new provocation
- "did to them what they did to me" (v. 11) — Samson's own defense reduces the entire conflict to a private cycle of tit-for-tat rather than any larger deliverance mission
- "the Spirit of the LORD came upon him in power" (v. 14) — the same empowering language used elsewhere for judges, here breaking into a story otherwise driven by personal vendetta

**Contrast structure**
- Samson's personal, almost petty grievance (his wife given to another) vs. the massive scale of destruction and death that follows (burned fields, slaughtered men, a thousand killed) — small personal offense escalates into major national conflict
- The men of Judah's fearful, compliant question, "don't you realize that the Philistines are rulers over us?" (v. 11) vs. Samson's singular, personal resistance to that same rule — the nation has fully accommodated oppression while one man, driven largely by personal motives, continues to resist it
- Samson's boastful strength ("I have killed a thousand men," v. 16) vs. his immediate, desperate weakness and thirst afterward (v. 18) — supernatural power and ordinary human frailty sit side by side without contradiction

**Notable expressions**
- v. 18 "must I now die of thirst" — after his greatest feat of strength, Samson's very next need is simply water, showing that even Spirit-empowered heroes remain fully human and dependent
- v. 19 "God opened up the hollow place" — the LORD graciously answers even a cry that arises from a man whose motives throughout the chapter have been largely personal rather than purely devotional
- v. 20 "Samson led Israel for twenty years" — a strikingly restrained summary statement, applied to a man whose "leadership" throughout this chapter looks far more like personal vendetta than covenantal deliverance in the mold of earlier judges

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the LORD's Spirit empower Samson (v. 14) even in the middle of a conflict driven so plainly by personal revenge rather than an appeal to deliver Israel from oppression?
   → It shows that God can accomplish his purposes (weakening Philistine dominance) even through a flawed human agent whose immediate motives are not primarily devotional; God's larger plan does not require pure motives in every human instrument he uses.

2. What does the men of Judah's willingness to hand Samson over to the Philistines (vv. 11-13) reveal about the spiritual and moral condition of Israel at this point in the story?
   → It shows a nation so thoroughly accustomed to oppression that they would rather protect their uneasy peace with the Philistines than support the one man resisting that oppression — comfortable accommodation to injustice has replaced any collective desire for deliverance.

3. Why does Samson's cry for water come immediately after his greatest display of strength (vv. 15-18), rather than at some more obviously vulnerable moment?
   → It underscores that supernatural empowerment for one task does not eliminate ordinary human need and limitation; even the most dramatic spiritual victories can be followed immediately by physical exhaustion and dependency.

4. What does it mean that God answers Samson's cry (v. 19) even though it is framed almost as a complaint or accusation ("must I now die of thirst") rather than a humble, worshipful request?
   → It reveals God's willingness to meet real, honest need even when it is expressed imperfectly or with an edge of self-focus, showing his compassion is not contingent on the polish of the request.

5. How does the chapter's final summary (v. 20) invite the reader to evaluate Samson's twenty years of "leading Israel" so far, given how personally motivated most of his actions have been?
   → It invites a critical, sober assessment — Samson's "leadership" bears little resemblance to Othniel's or Deborah's; his effectiveness against the Philistines seems to be almost incidental to his personal conflicts, raising the question of what kind of deliverer Israel actually has at this late stage in the book.

## Application

- Recognize that God can still use me for his larger purposes even in seasons when my own motives are mixed or not fully devotional, while not treating that as license to act from personal vendetta rather than genuine obedience.
- Examine whether comfortable accommodation to a difficult situation has quietly replaced any real desire in me for God's deliverance or change, the way it had for the men of Judah.
- Remember that even significant spiritual victories or accomplishments do not exempt me from ordinary human need; plan for rest and dependency rather than assuming strength in one moment means self-sufficiency in the next.
- Bring my real, even imperfectly expressed needs honestly to God, trusting that he responds to genuine need rather than requiring a perfectly composed prayer first.
