## Text (NIV)

**46** On hearing this, the citizens in the tower of Shechem went into the stronghold of the temple of El-Berith.
**47** When Abimelech heard that they had assembled there,
**48** he and all his men went up Mount Zalmon. He took an ax and cut off some branches, which he lifted to his shoulders. He ordered the men with him, "Quick! Do what you have seen me do!"
**49** So all the men cut branches and followed Abimelech. They piled them against the stronghold and set it on fire over the people inside. So all the people in the tower of Shechem, about a thousand men and women, also died.
**50** Next Abimelech went to Thebez and besieged it and captured it.
**51** Inside the city, however, was a strong tower, to which all the men and women-all the people of the city-fled. They locked themselves in and climbed up on the tower roof.
**52** Abimelech went to the tower and stormed it. But as he approached the entrance to the tower to set it on fire,
**53** a woman dropped an upper millstone on his head and cracked his skull.
**54** Hurriedly he called to his armor-bearer, "Draw your sword and kill me, so that they can't say, 'A woman killed him.'" So his servant ran him through, and he died.
**55** When the Israelites saw that Abimelech was dead, they went home.
**56** Thus God repaid the wickedness that Abimelech had done to his father by murdering his seventy brothers.
**57** God also made the men of Shechem pay for all their wickedness. The curse of Jotham son of Jerub-Baal came on them.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 46-49: The mass killing of about a thousand people sheltering in the tower of Shechem
- Verses 50-57: Abimelech's death at Thebez by a woman's hand, and the narrator's closing theological verdict

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "tower" (v. 46, 49, 51, 52) — repeated across both scenes, linking the destruction at Shechem's tower directly to Abimelech's own death at a strikingly similar tower in Thebez
- "so that they can't say, 'A woman killed him'" (v. 54) — Abimelech's dying concern is entirely about reputation, not repentance or reflection
- "repaid" / "made... pay" (v. 56-57) — the narrator's summary uses the language of just recompense twice, once for Abimelech and once for the men of Shechem

**Contrast structure**
- Abimelech's overwhelming, decisive violence against defenseless people in Shechem's tower (vv. 48-49) vs. his own sudden, almost accidental-seeming death from a single woman's dropped millstone (v. 53) — a dramatic reversal from perpetrator of mass killing to victim of an unplanned, singular blow
- Abimelech's desperate final act to avoid being remembered as killed by a woman (v. 54) vs. the narrator's calm indifference to that concern, plainly recording the true events and their theological meaning anyway — human vanity cannot rewrite the record God keeps
- The seemingly random woman's action (v. 53) vs. the narrator's explicit interpretation that this was God repaying Abimelech's specific crime (v. 56) — an ordinary, unplanned event is revealed to carry the full weight of divine justice

**Notable expressions**
- v. 48 "Quick! Do what you have seen me do!" — Abimelech's leadership by example here is chillingly effective, mobilizing an entire army instantly toward mass killing
- v. 54 Abimelech's final words reveal a man concerned to the last moment with his legacy and reputation among men, entirely untouched by any recognition of guilt or repentance for the massacre he had just committed
- v. 56-57 the narrator's closing verdict deliberately ties this entire violent episode back to Jotham's curse (9:20) and to the original crime against Gideon's seventy sons, framing the whole sequence as coherent divine justice rather than random chaos

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does Abimelech's death occur through such an ordinary, unplanned-seeming event (a woman dropping a millstone) rather than in a dramatic battle?
   → It underscores that the LORD's justice does not require grand or obviously miraculous means; an everyday object in an ordinary woman's hands is entirely sufficient to bring down a man who had built his power on mass murder, showing that no human strength is beyond God's reach.

2. What does Abimelech's final concern — avoiding the shame of being killed by a woman — reveal about the state of his heart even in his last moments?
   → It shows a man utterly consumed by reputation and honor to the very end, with no evidence of remorse for the massacre of his brothers or the citizens of the tower; his dying words are about how he will be remembered, not about what he has done.

3. Why does the narrator explicitly interpret both Abimelech's death and Shechem's destruction as God "repaying" and making them "pay" for specific wickedness (vv. 56-57), rather than leaving the events uninterpreted?
   → It insists that history is not simply a chain of political cause and effect but is under the moral governance of God, who holds both individual leaders and complicit communities accountable for specific sins, even when years pass before the reckoning comes.

4. Why is Jotham's earlier curse (9:20) explicitly recalled at the very end of this violent narrative (v. 57)?
   → It shows that the prophetic word spoken in chapter 9, though it may have seemed like empty rhetoric from a fleeing, powerless young man, was in fact accurate and was fulfilled in detail — God's justice may be delayed but it is not forgotten or nullified by time.

5. What is the overall theological point of this entire, brutal sequence (Abimelech's rise, his massacres, and his ignominious death) within the book of Judges?
   → It illustrates in the starkest terms what happens when a nation abandons the LORD's kingship for self-serving human power built on violence and manipulation — such power inevitably turns destructive and ultimately self-consuming, vindicating the LORD's justice even through the very evil it exposes.

## Application

- Remember that God's justice does not depend on dramatic or obvious means; ordinary circumstances and unremarkable people can be entirely sufficient instruments of his purposes.
- Examine whether concern for reputation or how I will be remembered ever outweighs genuine reflection on wrongdoing in my own life, as it did for Abimelech in his final moments.
- Take confidence that words of truth spoken faithfully, even from a position of apparent weakness (as with Jotham), are not wasted, even if their vindication takes time to unfold.
- Trust that no season of unchecked, violent, or manipulative power is ultimately beyond God's moral accounting, even when it seems to be winning in the short term.
