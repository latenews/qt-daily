## Text (NIV)

**11** From there they advanced against the people living in Debir (formerly called Kiriath Sepher).
**12** And Caleb said, "I will give my daughter Acsah in marriage to the man who attacks and captures Kiriath Sepher."
**13** Othniel son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother, took it; so Caleb gave his daughter Acsah to him in marriage.
**14** One day when she came to Othniel, she urged him to ask her father for a field. When she got off her donkey, Caleb asked her, "What can I do for you?"
**15** She replied, "Do me a special favor. Since you have given me land in the Negev, give me also springs of water." Then Caleb gave her the upper and lower springs.
**16** The descendants of Moses' father-in-law, the Kenite, went up from the City of Palms with the men of Judah to live among the people of the Desert of Judah in the Negev near Arad.
**17** Then the men of Judah went with the Simeonites their brothers and attacked the Canaanites living in Zephath, and they totally destroyed the city. Therefore it was called Hormah.
**18** The men of Judah also took Gaza, Ashkelon and Ekron-each city with its territory.
**19** The LORD was with the men of Judah. They took possession of the hill country, but they were unable to drive the people from the plains, because they had iron chariots.
**20** As Moses had promised, Hebron was given to Caleb, who drove from it the three sons of Anak.
**21** The Benjamites, however, failed to dislodge the Jebusites, who were living in Jerusalem; to this day the Jebusites live there with the Benjamites.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 11-16: The capture of Debir, Othniel and Acsah's marriage, and Acsah's bold request for springs of water
- Verses 17-21: Further victories by Judah and Simeon, followed by the first explicit note of incomplete conquest (v. 19, 21)

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "gave" (v. 13, 15, 20) — repeated across Caleb's giving of his daughter, his giving of the springs, and the LORD's giving of Hebron, tying human generosity to divine gift
- "the LORD was with" (v. 19) — a phrase of blessing that nonetheless sits directly beside the honest admission of failure that follows in the same verse
- "unable to drive... failed to dislodge" (v. 19, 21) — the first explicit acknowledgments of incomplete conquest, introduced almost casually within an otherwise successful account

**Contrast structure**
- Acsah's proactive, confident request ("do me a special favor... give me also springs of water," v. 15) vs. a more passive posture that might have simply accepted whatever land was given — she models initiative in seeking what is needed for flourishing, not just survival
- "the LORD was with the men of Judah" (v. 19) vs. "they were unable to drive the people from the plains, because they had iron chariots" — divine presence and blessing is stated in the very same breath as human limitation, without resolving the tension
- Caleb's complete success in driving out "the three sons of Anak" from Hebron (v. 20) vs. the Benjamites' failure regarding the Jebusites in Jerusalem (v. 21) — different tribes achieve markedly different degrees of faithfulness and success in the same campaign

**Notable expressions**
- v. 12-13 Caleb's marriage challenge and Othniel's success — this brief episode reintroduces Othniel, who will become Israel's first judge in chapter 3, subtly connecting his later leadership to an established pattern of courage and initiative
- v. 15 "springs of water" in addition to land already given — Acsah's request reveals discernment about what is truly needed for the land to be useful and fruitful, beyond the bare minimum already granted
- v. 19 "because they had iron chariots" — a matter-of-fact explanation for failure that raises an implicit question: does superior technology genuinely limit what the LORD who "was with" Judah could accomplish, or does it reveal something about Judah's own faith and effort?

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the narrative pause to include the smaller, personal story of Acsah's request for springs of water (vv. 14-15) within a chapter otherwise focused on military campaigns?
   → It highlights that faithful, wise initiative matters not only in dramatic conquest but also in the practical, ordinary pursuit of what is needed to actually thrive in what has already been given — the conquest of land means little without water to sustain it.

2. What does Acsah's boldness in approaching her father with a specific, well-reasoned request suggest as a model for approaching those with authority or resources?
   → She names her actual need clearly and respectfully rather than remaining silent or assuming she should be satisfied with less; the text presents this directness favorably, with Caleb granting her request without hesitation.

3. Why does the text state so plainly, within the same verse, both that "the LORD was with the men of Judah" and that "they were unable to drive the people from the plains" (v. 19)?
   → It refuses to offer an easy explanation, leaving open the tension between divine presence and human limitation — later chapters of Judges (2:1-3) will reveal that Israel's own incomplete obedience, not merely technological disadvantage, is the deeper issue behind these unfinished conquests.

4. Why might the text specifically note the Benjamites' failure regarding Jerusalem (v. 21), given how significant that city becomes later in Israel's history?
   → It sets up a long-term narrative tension — a portion of the very land promised to Israel remains only partially possessed for generations, a reminder that incomplete obedience in one era can have consequences extending far beyond it.

5. How does the reintroduction of Othniel in this chapter (vv. 12-13), well before his formal introduction as a judge in chapter 3, shape how the reader understands his later leadership?
   → It shows that Othniel's later effectiveness as Israel's first judge was not a sudden or random development, but grew out of an already-established pattern of courage and decisive action recognized within his family and tribe.

## Application

- Follow Acsah's example by clearly and respectfully naming what I actually need to flourish in what God has already given me, rather than passively accepting less out of hesitancy to ask.
- Sit honestly with the tension between God's presence and unfinished progress in my own life or ministry, resisting the urge to explain away every incomplete outcome with either simple blame or simple faith-language, and instead examining what my own obedience or effort may still lack.
- Consider what small, seemingly minor acts of unfinished obedience today might have consequences that extend far longer than I expect, as the Benjamites' failure with Jerusalem did.
- Notice how present character and courage, even in small or unofficial roles, often become the foundation for greater responsibility and leadership later.
