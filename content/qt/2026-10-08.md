## Text (NIV)

**11** When they were near Jebus and the day was almost gone, the servant said to his master, "Come, let's stop at this city of the Jebusites and spend the night."
**12** His master replied, "No. We won't go into an alien city, whose people are not Israelites. We will go on to Gibeah."
**13** He added, "Come, let's try to reach Gibeah or Ramah and spend the night in one of those places."
**14** So they went on, and the sun set as they neared Gibeah in Benjamin.
**15** There they stopped to spend the night. They went and sat in the city square, but no one took them into his home for the night.
**16** That evening an old man from the hill country of Ephraim, who was living in Gibeah (the men of the place were Benjamites), came in from his work in the fields.
**17** When he looked and saw the traveler in the city square, the old man asked, "Where are you going? Where did you come from?"
**18** He answered, "We are on our way from Bethlehem in Judah to a remote area in the hill country of Ephraim where I live. I have been to Bethlehem in Judah and now I am going to the house of the LORD. No one has taken me into his house.
**19** We have both straw and fodder for our donkeys and bread and wine for ourselves your servants-me, your maidservant, and the young man with us. We don't need anything."
**20** "You are welcome at my house," the old man said. "Let me supply whatever you need. Only don't spend the night in the square."
**21** So he took him into his house and fed his donkeys. After they had washed their feet, they had something to eat and drink.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 11-15: The Levite's deliberate choice to avoid a foreign city in favor of an Israelite one, only to find no hospitality among his own people
- Verses 16-21: An old man, himself a resident alien in Gibeah, finally offers the hospitality no native Benjamite would provide

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "spend the night" (v. 11, 13, 15) — repeated across the servant's suggestion, the Levite's plan, and their actual arrival, building anticipation toward the crucial question of where and with whom the night will be spent
- "no one took them into his home" / "no one has taken me into his house" (v. 15, 18) — repeated almost verbatim, emphasizing the shocking failure of Israelite hospitality specifically, in sharp contrast to what a foreign city might well have provided
- "we don't need anything" (v. 19) — the Levite's own insistence that he seeks no material help, only shelter, highlighting how minimal and reasonable a request is nonetheless refused

**Contrast structure**
- The Levite's confident refusal to lodge among the Jebusites because "its people are not Israelites" (v. 12) vs. the utter failure of hospitality he then receives from his own covenant people in Gibeah — an assumption that fellow Israelites would surely be safer and more welcoming proves tragically wrong
- The native Benjamites of Gibeah, who offer no welcome at all, vs. the old man, himself only a resident alien from Ephraim living among them, who alone extends genuine hospitality — an outsider embodies the hospitality that the actual community fails to provide
- The Levite's minimal, self-sufficient request ("we don't need anything," v. 19) vs. the old man's generous insistence, "let me supply whatever you need" (v. 20) — true hospitality exceeds what is even asked for

**Notable expressions**
- v. 12 "we won't go into an alien city, whose people are not Israelites" — a decision rooted in a reasonable assumption about safety among covenant people, which the rest of the chapter will devastatingly undermine
- v. 15 "they went and sat in the city square" — a vulnerable, exposed position for travelers, especially as night falls, made worse by nobody in an Israelite town offering shelter
- v. 20 "only don't spend the night in the square" — the old man's urgency about getting them off the street hints, even before it is explained, that something particularly dangerous characterizes this town after dark

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the Levite deliberately choose to avoid lodging with the Jebusites, "whose people are not Israelites" (v. 12), and what does the subsequent failure of hospitality in Gibeah suggest about that assumption?
   → His reasoning reflects an understandable but ultimately misplaced confidence that shared national and covenant identity would guarantee safety and welcome; the story exposes how deeply Israel's own moral condition had deteriorated, so that being "one of us" no longer reliably meant safety or basic decency.

2. Why does the narrator specifically note that no native Benjamite in Gibeah offers hospitality, and that it is instead an outsider — an Ephraimite resident alien — who finally does so (vv. 15-16)?
   → It sharply highlights the failure of the actual community to uphold basic covenantal hospitality, a serious social and religious obligation in this culture, while showing that genuine righteousness can be found in someone technically outside the local community rather than assumed simply because of shared residence or heritage.

3. What is the significance of the Levite's insistence that "we don't need anything" (v. 19), and how does this shape our understanding of what is actually being denied to him?
   → It underscores how minimal and reasonable his request truly was — merely a place to sleep, with all his own provisions already supplied — making the town's collective refusal to offer even this basic courtesy all the more striking and inexcusable.

4. Why might the old man's urgent insistence on getting the travelers off the street and into his house (v. 20) function as a subtle, ominous foreshadowing within the narrative?
   → His urgency suggests an awareness of real danger specific to this town after dark, planting unease in the reader's mind before the chapter's horrifying events are directly narrated, in a way that retrospectively makes his hospitality even more necessary and commendable.

5. What broader statement does this passage make about the reliability of shared identity or affiliation as a guarantee of safety, kindness, or moral behavior?
   → It challenges any assumption that belonging to the same nation, community, or even covenant people automatically ensures decent treatment; genuine righteousness and hospitality must be actively practiced and cannot be presumed simply from shared identity or label.

## Application

- Examine any assumption I hold that shared identity, background, or community membership automatically guarantees safety, kindness, or moral trustworthiness from others; genuine character must be discerned rather than presumed.
- Consider whether I have ever failed to extend basic hospitality or care to someone within my own community while perhaps being more cautious or generous toward outsiders, and examine what underlies that inconsistency.
- Follow the example of the old man in this passage by proactively offering generous hospitality that exceeds what is even requested, rather than providing only the minimum when someone is in need.
- Pay attention to a sense of unease or caution about a situation, even before fully understanding its source, and take reasonable steps to protect those who might be vulnerable, as the old man did in urging the travelers off the street.

## Prayer
