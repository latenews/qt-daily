## Text (NIV)

**1** These are the nations the LORD left to test all those Israelites who had not experienced any of the wars in Canaan
**2** (he did this only to teach warfare to the descendants of the Israelites who had not had previous battle experience):
**3** the five rulers of the Philistines, all the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites living in the Lebanon mountains from Mount Baal Hermon to Lebo Hamath.
**4** They were left to test the Israelites to see whether they would obey the LORD's commands, which he had given their forefathers through Moses.
**5** The Israelites lived among the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites and Jebusites.
**6** They took their daughters in marriage and gave their own daughters to their sons, and served their gods.
**7** The Israelites did evil in the eyes of the LORD; they forgot the LORD their God and served the Baals and the Asherahs.
**8** The anger of the LORD burned against Israel so that he sold them into the hands of Cushan-Rishathaim king of Aram Naharaim, to whom the Israelites were subject for eight years.
**9** But when they cried out to the LORD, he raised up for them a deliverer, Othniel son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother, who saved them.
**10** The Spirit of the LORD came upon him, so that he became Israel's judge and went to war. The LORD gave Cushan-Rishathaim king of Aram into the hands of Othniel, who overpowered him.
**11** So the land had peace for forty years, until Othniel son of Kenaz died.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 1-6: Why the nations were left in the land — to test Israel's obedience and to teach the new generation how to fight
- Verses 7-11: The first full cycle of the book — sin, sale into an enemy's hand, crying out, a deliverer raised up, rest for the land

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "test" (v. 1, 4) — the nations are explicitly framed as a test of obedience, not merely a military problem
- "did evil in the eyes of the LORD" (v. 7) — the book's signature diagnostic phrase, naming the root problem as covenant unfaithfulness, not bad luck
- "cried out to the LORD" (v. 9) — the turning point; distress itself is not what saves them, calling on the LORD is

**Contrast structure**
- v. 6 "took their daughters... served their gods" vs. v. 9 "cried out to the LORD" — the very people who intermarried and worshiped foreign gods are the ones the LORD still answers when they cry out, showing his mercy outruns their unfaithfulness
- v. 8 "sold them into the hands of" vs. v. 10 "gave... into the hands of Othniel" — the same phrase ("into the hands of") is used for both Israel's bondage and Israel's deliverance, showing the same sovereign hand behind judgment and rescue

**Notable expressions**
- v. 1-2 "to test... to teach warfare" — the LORD's purposes in leaving hardship are pedagogical: a generation that never fought must still learn dependence and courage
- v. 9 "Othniel son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother" — the first judge comes from a family already marked by faith (Caleb), suggesting deliverance often grows out of a legacy of faithfulness
- v. 10 "The Spirit of the LORD came upon him" — the earliest instance in Judges of this phrase, establishing that the judges' effectiveness is never mere human skill but Spirit-empowered

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the LORD deliberately leave hardship (the nations) in Israel's life instead of removing it, as the text says, "to test" and "to teach"?
   → Untested obedience and untried faith are shallow. The LORD values a people who choose loyalty when it costs something, and who learn to depend on him in real conflict rather than in comfort.

2. Why does the narrator move so quickly from "the Israelites did evil" to "the LORD gave them into the hands of..." — is God punishing them, or handing them over to consequences?
   → Both are true at once: it is judgment (the LORD's own righteous response to covenant-breaking) and it is also the natural outworking of forsaking the source of their strength. Sin does not just offend God; it hands people over to what they chose instead of him.

3. What does it mean that the very people who "served their gods" are the ones the LORD answers when they "cried out"?
   → It reveals that the LORD's rescue is never based on Israel's worthiness but on his covenant mercy. This anticipates the whole shape of grace: help comes not because the cry is pure, but because the One who hears is faithful.

4. Why is it significant that Othniel is introduced through his family line ("Caleb's younger brother") rather than through any personal exploit before this?
   → It shows deliverance is not manufactured by charisma or ambition; the LORD raises up whom he chooses, often from ordinary, faithful households, and it is his Spirit — not pedigree alone — that makes the judge effective (v. 10).

5. What is the significance of "forty years" of peace, and why does the text note the moment it ends ("until Othniel... died")?
   → Peace in Judges is always temporary and tied to a person, not yet to a lasting change of heart in the nation. The immediate mention of the judge's death signals that the cycle is about to repeat, exposing Israel's need for something more permanent than a human deliverer.

## Application

- When I notice "testing" circumstances in my life that never seem to go away, ask whether God intends them to teach me dependence and obedience rather than simply waiting for them to be removed.
- Examine what "foreign gods" quietly compete for my loyalty today — comfort, approval, security — and name them honestly instead of assuming unfaithfulness is only a past-tense problem.
- Remember that crying out to the LORD is available even after a long pattern of drifting; his mercy is not conditioned on having earned it first.
- Give thanks for the "Othniels" in my own story — people whose quiet, Spirit-empowered faithfulness became the means of my deliverance — and ask to become that kind of person for someone else.

## Prayer

Forgive me for the quiet ways I have drifted toward other gods — comfort, approval, control — even while calling you my God. Thank you that your mercy runs ahead of my unfaithfulness, and that you still answer when I cry out. Raise up in me, and through me, the kind of Spirit-filled faithfulness that Othniel showed, so that others might find rest because you were at work in me. Amen.
