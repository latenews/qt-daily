## Text (NIV)

**1** Now the Ephraimites asked Gideon, "Why have you treated us like this? Why didn't you call us when you went to fight Midian?" And they criticized him sharply.
**2** But he answered them, "What have I accomplished compared to you? Aren't the gleanings of Ephraim's grapes better than the full grape harvest of Abiezer?
**3** God gave Oreb and Zeeb, the Midianite leaders, into your hands. What was I able to do compared to you?" At this, their resentment against him subsided.
**4** Gideon and his three hundred men, exhausted yet keeping up the pursuit, came to the Jordan and crossed it.
**5** He said to the men of Succoth, "Give my troops some bread; they are worn out, and I am still pursuing Zebah and Zalmunna, the kings of Midian."
**6** But the officials of Succoth said, "Do you already have the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna in your possession? Why should we give bread to your troops?"
**7** Then Gideon replied, "Just for that, when the LORD has given Zebah and Zalmunna into my hand, I will tear your flesh with desert thorns and briers."
**8** From there he went up to Peniel and made the same request of them, but they answered as the men of Succoth had.
**9** So he said to the men of Peniel, "When I return in triumph, I will tear down this tower."
**10** Now Zebah and Zalmunna were in Karkor with a force of about fifteen thousand men, all that were left of the armies of the eastern peoples; a hundred and twenty thousand swordsmen had fallen.
**11** Gideon went up by the route of the nomads east of Nobah and Jogbehah and fell upon the unsuspecting army.
**12** Zebah and Zalmunna, the two kings of Midian, fled, but he pursued them and captured them, routing their entire army.
**13** Gideon son of Joash then returned from the battle by the Pass of Heres.
**14** He caught a young man of Succoth and questioned him, and the young man wrote down for him the names of the seventy-seven officials of Succoth, the elders of the town.
**15** Then Gideon came and said to the men of Succoth, "Here are Zebah and Zalmunna, about whom you taunted me by saying, 'Do you already have the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna in your possession? Why should we give bread to your exhausted men?'"
**16** He took the elders of the town and taught the men of Succoth a lesson by punishing them with desert thorns and briers.
**17** He also pulled down the tower of Peniel and killed the men of the town.
**18** Then he asked Zebah and Zalmunna, "What kind of men did you kill at Tabor?" "Men like you," they answered, "each one with the bearing of a prince."
**19** Gideon replied, "Those were my brothers, the sons of my own mother. As surely as the LORD lives, if you had spared their lives, I would not kill you."
**20** Turning to Jether, his oldest son, he said, "Kill them!" But Jether did not draw his sword, because he was only a boy and was afraid.
**21** Zebah and Zalmunna said, "Come, do it yourself. 'As is the man, so is his strength.'" So Gideon stepped forward and killed them, and took the ornaments off their camels' necks.

## Observation

**Structure (3 parts)**
- Verses 1-3: Gideon diffuses Ephraim's resentment with a diplomatic, self-effacing answer
- Verses 4-12: The exhausted pursuit continues; Succoth and Peniel refuse help, and Gideon captures the two Midianite kings
- Verses 13-21: Gideon returns to punish Succoth and Peniel, then personally executes Zebah and Zalmunna in an act of blood vengeance

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "gave... into" (v. 3) — even in his diplomatic answer to Ephraim, Gideon insists that God, not any man, gave the victory
- "Why should we give bread" (v. 6, echoed in v. 15) — Succoth's mocking refusal is later thrown back at them almost verbatim when Gideon returns victorious
- "exhausted" / "worn out" (v. 4, 15) — repeated to underscore how costly and draining the ongoing pursuit was, making Succoth's refusal to help more striking

**Contrast structure**
- Gideon's gracious, humble answer to Ephraim's harsh accusation (vv. 1-3) vs. his harsh, punitive response to Succoth and Peniel's refusal to help (vv. 7, 9, 16-17) — the same man responds very differently depending on who is confronting him and what is at stake
- Succoth's mocking question "do you already have the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna" (v. 6) vs. Gideon's literal fulfillment of exactly that condition upon his return (v. 15) — their sarcasm becomes their own undoing
- Gideon's earlier hesitance and need for signs (ch. 6-7) vs. his now-decisive, even brutal, personal execution of the two kings (v. 21) — the same man has moved from timidity to unflinching, and ethically troubling, resolve

**Notable expressions**
- v. 2 "the gleanings of Ephraim's grapes better than the full grape harvest of Abiezer" — a skillful, humble proverb that redirects credit to Ephraim's smaller contribution (capturing the leaders) rather than defending his own larger role
- v. 9 "I will tear down this tower" — Gideon's threats, and later actions, escalate from denying bread to promising violent retribution, revealing a harsher side to his leadership under strain
- v. 19 "those were my brothers" — Gideon's personal motive for killing the kings is revealed only at the end, reframing the execution as blood vengeance for his own family rather than only justice for the nation

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does Gideon respond so differently to Ephraim's sharp criticism (with humility and diplomacy) than he does to Succoth and Peniel's refusal to help (with threats and later violence)?
   → It may reflect the different stakes involved — internal tribal jealousy required tact to preserve unity, while what Gideon perceived as a betrayal in the heat of a costly, life-or-death pursuit provoked a harsher, more personal response; it also exposes that Gideon's character is not uniformly gracious, but responsive to circumstance and personal cost.

2. Why does Succoth's sarcastic taunt ("do you already have the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna," v. 6) become the very words Gideon throws back at them in verse 15?
   → It shows the poetic justice built into the narrative — those who mocked the possibility of Gideon's success are confronted with the literal fulfillment of what they doubted, and their own words return to indict them.

3. What does it reveal about Gideon that his final motive for killing Zebah and Zalmunna is personal ("those were my brothers," v. 19) rather than purely about delivering Israel?
   → It complicates a straightforwardly heroic reading of Gideon; his actions here are entangled with personal vengeance, suggesting that even a Spirit-empowered deliverer can act from mixed, and not fully righteous, motives.

4. Why does Jether, Gideon's own son, refuse to kill the kings, and why might Gideon have asked him to do it in the first place?
   → Asking his young son may have been intended as an insult to the captured kings (to be killed by a mere boy) or as a way to pass on the "honor" of decisive leadership; the boy's fear shows an honest, human reluctance to take a life, in contrast to his father's hardened resolve.

5. What does this chapter's mixture of humility (toward Ephraim) and harsh vengeance (toward Succoth, Peniel, and the kings) suggest about the danger of unchecked power after a great victory?
   → Success and momentum can subtly shift a leader's posture from dependence on God to a sense of personal entitlement to retribution; the same Gideon who once needed fleece for reassurance now acts with unquestioned, even excessive, authority.

## Application

- Notice how differently I tend to respond to criticism depending on who delivers it and what is at stake for me personally; ask God to give me the same humility toward those who wrong me that I show toward those I need to keep on good terms with.
- Be alert to how success, momentum, or exhaustion in a difficult season can shift my responses from grace toward harshness, especially toward those who fail to support me when I need it.
- Examine whether any of my own strong reactions are driven by personal grievance dressed up as righteous justice, as Gideon's vengeance for his brothers was mixed with his role as Israel's deliverer.
- Consider that "victory" or vindication does not automatically sanctify everything I do afterward; continue to submit even my justified anger and actions to God's scrutiny.

## Prayer

Lord, thank you for the moments you have given me grace and wisdom to respond humbly to criticism, as Gideon did with Ephraim. Forgive me for the times exhaustion, success, or personal grievance have hardened my heart toward others, leading me to respond with more harshness than your justice actually requires. Search my motives when I feel most justified in anger or vengeance, and keep me from mistaking personal retribution for righteous action. Teach me to remain dependent on you even after you have given me victory, rather than assuming victory gives me license to act as I please. Amen.
