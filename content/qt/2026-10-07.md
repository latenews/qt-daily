## Text (NIV)

**1** In those days Israel had no king. Now a Levite who lived in a remote area in the hill country of Ephraim took a concubine from Bethlehem in Judah.
**2** But she was unfaithful to him. She left him and went back to her father's house in Bethlehem, Judah. After she had been there four months,
**3** her husband went to her to persuade her to return. He had with him his servant and two donkeys. She took him into her father's house, and when her father saw him, he gladly welcomed him.
**4** His father-in-law, the girl's father, prevailed upon him to stay; so he remained with him three days, eating and drinking, and sleeping there.
**5** On the fourth day they got up early and he prepared to leave, but the girl's father said to his son-in-law, "Refresh yourself with something to eat; then you can go."
**6** So the two of them sat down to eat and drink together. Afterward the girl's father said, "Please stay tonight and enjoy yourself."
**7** And when the man got up to go, his father-in-law persuaded him, so he stayed there that night.
**8** On the morning of the fifth day, when he rose to go, the girl's father said, "Refresh yourself. Wait till afternoon!" So the two of them ate together.
**9** Then when the man, with his concubine and his servant, got up to leave, his father-in-law, the girl's father, said, "Now look, it's almost evening. Spend the night here; the day is nearly over. Stay and enjoy yourself. Early tomorrow morning you can get up and be on your way home."
**10** But, unwilling to stay another night, the man left and went toward Jebus (that is, Jerusalem), with his two saddled donkeys and his concubine.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verse 1: A brief, loaded introduction naming Israel's leaderless condition and the Levite's concubine
- Verses 2-10: An extended, almost leisurely account of hospitality and delay at the father-in-law's house, ending in the Levite's decision to finally depart

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "in those days Israel had no king" (v. 1) — repeated from 17:6 and 18:1, again signaling that what follows should be read as a further consequence of this leaderless condition
- "stay" / "refresh yourself" / "enjoy yourself" (vv. 4-9, repeated across multiple days) — the father-in-law's hospitality is described in unusually generous, repetitive detail, delaying the narrative's forward movement
- "unwilling to stay another night" (v. 10) — the turning point where the Levite's own decision, after so much urging to remain, sets the tragic events of the rest of the chapter into motion

**Contrast structure**
- The concubine's earlier unfaithfulness and departure (v. 2) vs. her father's warm, repeated welcome and generous hospitality toward the Levite who has come to retrieve her (vv. 3-9) — reconciliation and warmth mark this first part of the story, before the darkness that follows
- The father-in-law's persistent urging to stay longer (repeated across days 3, 4, and 5) vs. the Levite's final refusal to stay even one more night (v. 10) — an abundance of caution and delay is followed by a fateful, ordinary decision to press on that will prove disastrous
- The extended, almost peaceful domestic scene in Bethlehem vs. the reader's awareness (from the chapter's opening framing) that this peaceful interlude occurs within a nation without king or order — surface calm belies the underlying societal instability

**Notable expressions**
- v. 1 "a Levite... took a concubine" — introduces a member of the priestly tribe whose personal life, like Micah's private priest before him, will be caught up in the moral disorder characteristic of this section of Judges
- v. 6-9 the father-in-law's repeated, almost desperate hospitality — its unusual length in the narrative creates a lingering, uneasy tension, as the reader senses that something significant hinges on when the Levite finally leaves
- v. 10 the plain, unremarkable decision to leave in the late afternoon — an entirely ordinary choice about timing that will have devastating, unforeseen consequences later in the chapter

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does the narrator spend so much space on the father-in-law's repeated, generous hospitality (vv. 4-9) before the story's darker events begin?
   → The extended, almost peaceful domestic scene creates a stark before-and-after contrast with what follows, and its very length builds a subtle tension — the reader senses that timing and decisions in this unremarkable stretch of days will matter more than they first appear to.

2. Why does the text again note "in those days Israel had no king" (v. 1) at the very start of this new, troubling narrative?
   → It signals to the reader that this account, like the Micah and Danite narrative before it, should be read as another consequence of Israel's leaderless, uncentralized condition — personal and moral chaos flourishes without any shared covenantal authority.

3. What is the significance of the Levite's decision to leave "unwilling to stay another night" (v. 10), given how much hospitality and urging to remain preceded it?
   → It shows how an entirely reasonable, even understandable decision (wanting to finally get on the road after days of delay) can set in motion consequences no one could have anticipated — the story does not portray this choice as morally wrong, yet it becomes the hinge point for tragedy.

4. Why might the concubine's earlier unfaithfulness and the couple's apparent reconciliation (vv. 2-4) be included as backstory before the more consequential events that follow?
   → It establishes her as a real, complex person within a strained relationship, setting up the reader to feel the full weight of what will happen to her in the rest of the chapter, rather than allowing her to remain a mere plot device.

5. How does this passage's ordinary, domestic tone function within the larger, disturbing arc of the final chapters of Judges?
   → It lulls the reader into a sense of normalcy and warmth, making the abrupt shift into horror in the following verses even more jarring and effective as a picture of how quickly and unexpectedly disorder can erupt even from ordinary circumstances.

## Application

- Recognize that a season of genuine warmth, reconciliation, or hospitality does not guarantee safety or a smooth continuation; remain spiritually alert even during peaceful, ordinary stretches of life.
- Consider that seemingly small, reasonable decisions (like the Levite's choice about when to leave) can have consequences far beyond what is visible at the time; bring even ordinary decisions before God rather than assuming they carry no weight.
- Take seriously that a lack of shared, godly accountability and leadership in a family, community, or nation tends to create the conditions where personal moral chaos can flourish unchecked.
- Notice and value the personhood and dignity of those often treated as background figures in a larger story, following the narrator's example of giving the concubine real context and complexity before the harder events unfold.
