## Text (NIV)

**12** Then Jephthah sent messengers to the Ammonite king with the question: "What do you have against us that you have attacked our country?"
**13** The king of the Ammonites answered Jephthah's messengers, "When Israel came up out of Egypt, they took away my land from the Arnon to the Jabbok, all the way to the Jordan. Now give it back peaceably."
**14** Jephthah sent back messengers to the Ammonite king,
**15** saying: "This is what Jephthah says: Israel did not take the land of Moab or the land of the Ammonites.
**16** But when they came up out of Egypt, Israel went through the desert to the Red Sea and on to Kadesh.
**17** Then Israel sent messengers to the king of Edom, saying, 'Give us permission to go through your country,' but the king of Edom would not listen. They sent also to the king of Moab, and he refused. So Israel stayed at Kadesh.
**18** "Next they traveled through the desert, skirted the lands of Edom and Moab, passed along the eastern side of the country of Moab, and camped on the other side of the Arnon. They did not enter the territory of Moab, for the Arnon was its border.
**19** "Then Israel sent messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites, who ruled in Heshbon, and said to him, 'Let us pass through your country to our own place.'
**20** Sihon, however, did not trust Israel to pass through his territory. He mustered all his men and encamped at Jahaz and fought with Israel.
**21** "Then the LORD, the God of Israel, gave Sihon and all his men into Israel's hands, and they defeated them. Israel took over all the land of the Amorites who lived in that country,
**22** capturing all of it from the Arnon to the Jabbok and from the desert to the Jordan.
**23** "Now since the LORD, the God of Israel, has driven the Amorites out before his people Israel, what right have you to take it over?
**24** Will you not take what your god Chemosh gives you? Likewise, whatever the LORD our God has given us, we will possess.
**25** Are you better than Balak son of Zippor, king of Moab? Did he ever quarrel with Israel or fight with them?
**26** For three hundred years Israel occupied Heshbon, Aroer, the surrounding settlements and all the towns along the Arnon. Why didn't you retake them during that time?
**27** I have not wronged you, but you are doing me wrong by waging war against me. Let the LORD, the Judge, decide the dispute this day between the Israelites and the Ammonites."
**28** The king of Ammon, however, paid no attention to the message Jephthah sent him.

## Observation

**Structure (2 parts)**
- Verses 12-13: An initial exchange — Jephthah's direct question and the Ammonite king's territorial claim
- Verses 14-28: Jephthah's extended historical, legal, and theological rebuttal, ending in the king's flat refusal to listen

**Key repeated words/phrases**
- "give it back" / "give... over" (v. 13, 24) — the dispute is framed from the start as a question of rightful possession, which Jephthah answers point by point
- "the LORD, the God of Israel" (v. 21, 23) — invoked twice at the heart of Jephthah's argument, anchoring Israel's claim to the land not in conquest alone but in the LORD's own action and gift
- "did not... did he ever... why didn't you" (vv. 15, 25-26) — a string of rhetorical questions builds a legal-style case, piling up evidence before Jephthah's concluding appeal to divine judgment

**Contrast structure**
- The Ammonite king's short, simple demand ("give it back peaceably," v. 13) vs. Jephthah's long, detailed historical rebuttal (vv. 15-27) — a serious, well-reasoned response answering a comparatively careless accusation
- "Will you not take what your god Chemosh gives you? Likewise... we will possess" (v. 24) — Jephthah reasons within the Ammonite king's own religious logic without necessarily endorsing it, exposing the inconsistency of contesting Israel's God-given land while assuming the same right for Chemosh
- Three hundred years of unchallenged Israelite occupation (v. 26) vs. the king's sudden present claim — Jephthah highlights how implausible the accusation is given such a long, undisputed history

**Notable expressions**
- v. 24 "your god Chemosh" — Jephthah does not necessarily affirm Chemosh's reality or legitimacy, but uses the king's own theological framework to expose the double standard in his complaint
- v. 27 "Let the LORD, the Judge, decide the dispute this day" — Jephthah ultimately submits the entire question to divine judgment rather than resting only on his own argument, showing where his final confidence lies
- v. 28 "paid no attention" — the extensive, carefully constructed case is met with simple, dismissive indifference, showing that thorough argument does not guarantee a hearing

## Interpretation (Questions)

1. Why does Jephthah attempt a lengthy diplomatic and historical argument (vv. 15-27) before resorting to war, especially given his own history of being treated unfairly by his family?
   → It shows Jephthah pursuing rightful, reasoned means of resolving conflict first, seeking peace through truth and legitimate claim rather than assuming violence is the only option — a notable contrast to some other episodes of impulsive violence in Judges.

2. What is the significance of Jephthah citing three hundred years of unchallenged occupation (v. 26) as part of his case?
   → It underscores how baseless and untimely the Ammonite king's claim is; a right supposedly violated should not need three centuries to be raised, exposing the claim as opportunistic rather than genuinely aggrieved.

3. Why does Jephthah reason using the Ammonite king's own god, Chemosh (v. 24), rather than only appealing to the LORD directly?
   → It is a rhetorical strategy that meets the king on his own terms, showing the inconsistency of his position even by his own religious logic — if territorial gain from one's own god is legitimate for the Ammonites, then Israel's God-given possession should be recognized as equally legitimate by the same standard.

4. What does it mean for Jephthah to conclude by submitting the entire dispute to "the LORD, the Judge" (v. 27) rather than resting his case only on historical or diplomatic argument?
   → It reveals where Jephthah's ultimate confidence lies — not merely in the strength of his own reasoning, but in trusting that God himself will vindicate what is right, especially once human negotiation has been exhausted.

5. Why does the passage end so simply, with the king "paying no attention" to such a carefully constructed case (v. 28)?
   → It shows that even the most reasonable, truthful argument will not always be received, and it sets up the necessity of the coming conflict — some conflicts cannot be resolved through words alone when the other party refuses to engage honestly.

## Application

- Before assuming that conflict must be resolved by force or estrangement, consider whether there is a reasoned, honest case to be made first, as Jephthah attempted with the Ammonite king.
- When someone applies a standard to me that they do not apply consistently to themselves, it is fair, as Jephthah did, to name that inconsistency clearly rather than simply accepting an unfair accusation.
- In disputes where I have done what is right and been thorough and fair, be prepared for the possibility that the other party may simply refuse to listen — my responsibility is faithfulness in the process, not control over the outcome.
- Ultimately entrust unresolved conflicts to God as the true Judge, especially once legitimate efforts at honest resolution have been exhausted.
